Wreck

The Mary Celestia

History
Built in New York in 1854, the paddle steamer Mary Celestia was typical of the sleek, fast vessels built to evade the Union blockade during the American Civil War. She was captained by the master mariner Thomas Pickering of Key West, Florida, who made several trips from Nassau to Bermuda carrying contraband goods and returning with cotton intended for European markets. On her final voyage in September 1864, the Mary Celestia ran aground and sank off the south shore of Bermuda, carrying a cargo of munitions, canned goods, and wine. The wreck is one of Bermuda's premier dive sites. The bow section was heavily salvaged in the aftermath, but a significant portion of the hull remains intact on the seabed in about 55 feet of water.
  • Massive anchor, paddle wheels, boilers, and machinery components visible in the debris field
  • Iron parts (boilers, engine framing, paddle wheels) stand out vividly against white sand
  • Hurricanes occasionally shift sands, revealing new artifacts
  • Rare unopened bottle of wine from 1864 discovered in 2011 — made international headlines
  • One of the best-preserved Civil War-era shipwrecks in the Atlantic
  • Popular destination for both recreational divers and marine archaeologists
Current Condition
Sitting in 55 feet of blue water, the remains are spread across a sandy seabed, providing excellent visibility for diving. While the wooden portions have deteriorated, the iron parts — such as the massive boilers, the framing of the engine, and the paddle wheels — stand out vividly against the white sand. In recent years, hurricanes have occasionally shifted the sands, revealing new artifacts. In 2011, a rare unopened bottle of wine from 1864 was discovered during a routine dive, making international headlines. The site is protected as a historic maritime site, and spearfishing is strictly prohibited within the zone. Despite being more than a century and a half underwater, the wreck remains one of the best-preserved Civil War-era shipwrecks in the Atlantic. Divers can easily explore the debris field, where a massive anchor, paddle wheels, boilers, and machinery components remain visible.
