The Sea Venture
Wreck · 1609
The flagship of the London Company, bound for Jamestown. Ran aground on Bermuda's reefs in 1609, giving the island its first permanent English settlers.
Depth: ~12–18 ftJamestown's survivors

Wreck · Scuttled 1984
A former U.S. Coast Guard buoy tender bought for $1 and scuttled to become an artificial reef. Lies almost fully intact in 75–80 ft near Horseshoe Bay.
Depth: 75–80 ftOne of Bermuda's most popular dives

Iristo (Aristo)
Wreck
Steam freighter built in 1918 in Lorain, Ohio. Originally the Lake Jessup. Ran aground on Bermuda's north shore in 1919.
L'Herminie
Wreck
First-class 60-gun French frigate, launched 1824. One of Bermuda's most impressive warship wrecks — sunk in 1838.
Mills Breaker Wrecks
Wreck
Multiple wrecks at the Mills Breaker reef site. The Beaumarais Castle, an iron-hulled cargo sailing vessel from Calcutta, was one of them.
North Rock Shipwrecks
Wreck
North Rock is Bermuda's biggest reef preserve; at least two shipwrecks lie near it, possibly many more. Includes the ship that brought the first recorded Briton to Bermuda.
Depth: Not specified
The Airplane
Wreck
Not all Bermuda wrecks are ships — this site was long thought to be a B-29 bomber that went down after taking off from Bermuda.
Depth: 21–25 ft
The Blanche King
Wreck
Sank in December 1920 en route from Norfolk to Bermuda with a cargo of coal. Grounded on the reef off the north shore.
The Caeser
Wreck
English brigantine built in 1814 in Durham. Lost on a reef off Bermuda's west end on May 17, 1818, en route from Shields, England to Bermuda.
The Caraquet
Wreck
British Mail packet and passenger steamer. Sank June 25, 1923 after hitting rough seas — a strong current pushed her further than the captain calculated.
Depth: 45 ft · 200 ft long
The Constellation
Wreck
Four-masted wooden schooner built 1918 in Maine. Originally the Sally Persis Noyes. Later a cruise ship and WWII cargo vessel. Now a shallow wreck off the north shore.
Depth: 26–30 ft · 1,034 tons
The Corinthian
Wreck
Originally built by Shell Oil as an oil tender for Gulf of Mexico rigs. Refitted and arrived in Bermuda in 2009 before sinking.
The Cristobal Colon
Wreck
Luxury cruise liner built in 1923 for the Transatlantic Spanish Line. The largest shipwreck in Bermuda waters.
The Curlew
Wreck
Iron-hulled, three-masted English steamer on the Halifax–Bermuda–St. Thomas route. Sank three years after being bought by Cunard.
The Darlington
Wreck
Sturdy iron-hulled steamer built in 1881 at Swan & Hunter in Newcastle. Sank in December 1886 on a reef off Bermuda's west end.
The Forceful
Wreck
Purposefully sunk in 2009 as part of the Bermuda Intact Wreck Initiative. The larger of two BIWI wrecks, sitting in about 60 ft off the south shore.
Depth: Max 60 ft
The Gleana
Wreck
Also known as "Kevin's Wreck" — a converted salvage schooner lost by the Lusher Brothers during salvage of the Minnie Breslauer.
The Hermes
Wreck
Former U.S. Coast Guard buoy tender, bought for $1 and scuttled in 1984. Lies nearly intact in 75–80 ft south of Warwick Long Bay.
Depth: 75–80 ft · 165 ft long
The Kate
Wreck
Iron-hulled English steam freighter built in 1874 in Whitby. En route from Galveston to Le Havre when she ran aground on Bermuda's north shore.
The King
Wreck
Navy tug boat built in 1941 in South Carolina. Brought to Bermuda for dive and treasure salvage work, then donated and scuttled as a dive site.
The Lartington
Wreck
Tramp steamer built in 1875 in Sunderland, England, by Short Brothers. Used both sail and steam. Ran aground on Bermuda's north shore.
The Madiana
Wreck
Steamship built in 1877 by R. Napier & Sons in Glasgow. Originally the Balmoral Castle — an iron-hulled transatlantic liner for passengers and mail.
The Manilla
Wreck
Unknown Dutchman-class ship, dubbed "the Manilla" by archaeologists. Believed to have run aground in 1753 at what is now North East Breaker.
The Mary Celestia
Wreck
Blockade runner that sank in 1864 at age two. Operated out of Bermuda during the U.S. Civil War. One of the most famous Bermuda wrecks.
The Minnie Breslauer
Wreck
Steel-hulled English steam freighter built in 1872 in Portugal. Left Malaga in early 1873 on her maiden voyage to New York — never made it.
The Montana
Wreck
Side-paddlewheel steamer and Civil War blockade runner. Sank December 1863 on Bermuda's northwest reefs. Previously named Nola, Gloria, and Paramount.
Depth: ~42 ft · 750 tons
The North Carolina
Wreck
Iron-hulled three-masted square-rigger built in 1876 in Scotland. Owned and operated for the North Carolina — a Bermudian-owned vessel running goods between Bermuda and New York.
The Pelinaion
Wreck
Large cargo steamer built in 1907 at Port Glasgow, originally named Hill Glen. Changed ownership many times before being scuttled as an artificial reef in 1915.
The Pollockshields
Wreck
German cargo steamer built in 1890 in Hamburg. Captured by the British in WWI and renamed. Sank in 1918 carrying munitions — sits upright in 40 ft off Somerset.
Depth: 40 ft · 323 ft long
The Rita Zovetta
Wreck
Cargo steamer built in 1919 in Glasgow, originally the War Gascon. Outfitted to carry war supplies before being sold and renamed. Sank in Bermuda in 1920.
The Sea Venture Ferry
Wreck
A modern ferry boat named after the 1609 Sea Venture wreck. Relatively new, located at Eastern Blue Cut — not as interesting as the original, but a dive site nonetheless.
The Sea Venture
Wreck
17th-century armed merchantman, built 1609 in Suffolk, England. Flagship of the London Company bound for Jamestown. Ran aground in Bermuda in 1609 — survivors founded the first English settlement.
The Stonewall
Wreck
Unidentified 17th-century wooden barque. Named for the stone ballast that stretched across the seabed when first discovered by divers.
The Taunton
Wreck
Steel-hulled cargo steamer built in 1902 in Copenhagen. Chartered to United Fruit Company. Sank in Bermuda waters after running aground.
The Virginia Merchant
Wreck
English wooden sailing ship owned by the Virginia Company, under Captain Robert Burke. Carrying 17th-century supplies when it ran aground in Bermuda in 1619.
The Warwick
Wreck
English merchantman brigantine, preparing to leave Castle Harbour in November 1619 with badly needed supplies for Jamestown. Sank in a hurricane.
The Xing Da
Wreck
Modern fishing vessel. UC San Diego faculty, researchers, and students collaborate with Bermuda's Department of Environment and Natural Resources on this site.
Castle Harbour and Nonsuch Buoy
Reef
Southeast corner of Bermuda. The Castle Harbour Islands Nature Reserve contains all islands and islets across the mouth of Castle Harbour.
Cathedral Dive Site
Reef
Popular reef dive near Gurnet Rock off the East End. A huge underwater arch — a favourite of the dive shops at this end of the island.
Eastern Blue Cut
Reef
One of the largest "cuts" or breaks in Bermuda's reef. Nutrient flow through the cut attracts fish and marine life year-round.
Hangover Hole
Reef
Exceptional canyons, swim-throughs, and a hard-to-find underwater cave. Nearby the Gleana wreck. Challenging — not for novice divers.
Hog Breaker
Reef
Open dive site close to the Caraquet wreck. Protected and buoyed, with great swim-throughs and overhangs. Popular with divers.
North East Breaker
Reef
Protected area around the North East Breaker buoy. What's left of multiple shipwrecks — including the 18th-century Manilla — lies scattered across the site.
North Rock
Reef
About seven miles from the east end of Bermuda. One of the crown jewels of the reef system — coarse pink sand, coral, purple sea fans, and crystal-clear water from 3 to 30 ft.
Depth: 3–30 ft
Sandy Hole
Reef
The solid reef that has stopped many ships dead in the water over 400 years. Buoyed dive site, depth 6 to 33 ft.
Shell City / Hammerhead / Barracuda
Reef
Collection of shallow dive sites in the same area — open areas for novice divers and second-reef dives. Named for the marine life that hangs out there.
Snake Pit
Reef
Protected dive site with a large bowl of pure white sand surrounded by healthy coral patch reef. Incredible visibility on the high tide, year-round.
South West Breaker
Reef
Southern-most breaker reef in Bermuda, a protected buoyed dive site. Not a coral formation or rock — a chunk of coral-encrusted rock jutting from the sea floor.
Tarpon Hole
Reef
Reef full of massive overhangs, caves, and tunnels. Hosts large concentrations of tarpon — at certain times of year they can be seen schooling here.
